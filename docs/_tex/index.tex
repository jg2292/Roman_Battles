\begin{abstract}
This study examines the spatial distribution of Roman battles and
investigates the relationship between military conflict, settlement
patterns, territorial boundaries, and specialized infrastructure across
the Roman world. Using georeferenced battle locations, historical
territorial boundary reconstructions, and settlement records from the
Pleiades Gazetteer, the analysis applies spatial statistical methods and
Monte Carlo simulations to evaluate whether the distribution of battles
can be explained by broader geographic patterns. The results indicate
that Roman battles were significantly more spatially clustered than
expected under spatial randomness, and that this clustering persisted
after accounting for the distribution of known settlements. Battle
locations were also substantially closer to historical territorial
perimeters than expected under the settlement-based model, although the
strength of this relationship varied across alternative distance-decay
assumptions and between external and civil conflicts. The infrastructure
analysis further identifies differences between conflict
classifications, with civil conflicts exhibiting closer proximity to
entertainment facilities and ports, while external battles were closer
to forts and entertainment facilities than expected. Taken together,
these findings suggest that the spatial geography of Roman warfare
cannot be explained by a single geographic relationship. Rather, the
distribution of battles reflects several overlapping spatial patterns
whose importance varies according to the type and historical context of
the conflict.
\end{abstract}


\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

The study of Rome sits as a well worn topic in the humanities going all
the way back to the Renessaince and argueably down to the fall of the
empire itself. Warfare was a fundamental component of the political,
territorial, and administrative development of the Roman world and has
take up a large component of the aforementioned study. Military
engagements occurred across a geographically extensive territory,
ranging from the Italian peninsula and western Mediterranean to the
frontiers of northern Europe, North Africa, and the eastern
Mediterranean. Although the historical circumstances of individual
battles have received considerable scholarly attention, their collective
geographic distribution has been understudied. Utilizing geographical
statistical modeling offers an avenue to build upon past strategic
history on the Roman Empire.

Few historians would claim that battle locations were distributed
randomly across the landscape on a qualitative basis, but such a claim
has not yet been tested on a quantitative basis. Similarly, military
engagements may have been influenced by the location of established
settlements, the boundaries of Roman territorial control, and the
presence of specialized infrastructure such as forts. Internal conflicts
may exhibit different geographic characteristics from conflicts
involving external opponents.

By combining georeferenced battle records with reconstructed historical
boundaries and settlement data, it is possible to evaluate whether
observed battle locations exhibit spatial patterns consistent with
particular geographic features. This study investigates the spatial
distribution of Roman battles through a series of spatial statistical
tests and Monte Carlo simulations. Rather than assuming that a single
geographic factor explains the location of military engagements, the
analysis compares several alternative spatial models.

The primary objectives are:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  Determine whether Roman battle locations exhibit significant spatial
  clustering relative to spatial randomness.
\item
  Evaluate whether the distribution of battles is associated with the
  historical distribution of settlements.
\item
  Investigate whether battles occurred disproportionately close to the
  territorial boundaries of Roman control.
\item
  Examine whether external conflicts and civil conflicts exhibit
  different spatial relationships with military and civic
  infrastructure.
\end{enumerate}

By examining these relationships together, this study seeks to better
understand the geographic characteristics of Roman warfare and the
relationship between military activity and the historical Roman
landscape.

\section{Literature Review}\label{literature-review}

\subsection{The Grand Strategy of the Roman
Empire}\label{the-grand-strategy-of-the-roman-empire}

In the seminal work \emph{The Grand Strategy of the Roman Empire},
Luttwak examines how Roman military organization and defensive strategy
evolved through time in response to the changing demands of the empire
(\citeproc{ref-luttwak2016grand}{Luttwak 2016}). His work emphasizes the
relationship between military deployment, frontier defense,
fortifications, and the broader territorial organization of Roman power.
Luttwak's interpretation of Roman strategy has been influential and it
provides a qualitative backdrop for the work in the paper that seeks to
consider how military activity was associated with the geography of
Roman control.

\subsection{GIS Analysis of the Roman Conquest of
Dacia}\label{gis-analysis-of-the-roman-conquest-of-dacia}

More recent archaeological research has demonstrated the value of
GIS-based spatial analysis for investigating these relationships at a
geographic scale. Oltean and Fonte use GIS, remote sensing, and spatial
modeling to examine the relationships between Roman military bases,
Dacian strongholds, and the surrounding landscape during the Roman
conquest of Dacia (\citeproc{ref-oltean2021gis}{Oltean and Fonte 2021}).
Their analysis considers how terrain, visibility, and potential movement
routes influenced the strategic positioning of military installations.
Their research shows how spatial analysis is a valid tool to evaluate
historical interpretations of Roman military activity and reveal
relationships between military infrastructure and the surrounding
landscape. While their research focuses on a particular campaign and
geographic region compared to this papers more broad inquiry, it
provides a direct precedent for using GIS to investigate the spatial
characteristics of Roman warfare.

\subsection{Modelling Spatial Heterogeneity in Archaeological
Landscapes}\label{modelling-spatial-heterogeneity-in-archaeological-landscapes}

The application of spatial analysis to archaeological distributions also
provides a broader foundation for this research. Bevan and Conolly
examine spatial heterogeneity in archaeological artifact distributions,
demonstrating that patterns of material remains can vary across
landscapes and that relationships between archaeological distributions
and environmental characteristics may not be geographically uniform
(\citeproc{ref-bevan2009modelling}{Bevan and Conolly 2009}). Their work
highlights the importance of accounting for underlying spatial structure
when interpreting the distribution of archaeological evidence. This is
relevant to the present study because the geographic distribution of
documented settlements provides an important baseline against which the
distribution of battle locations can be evaluated. Rather than assuming
that battles occurred independently of existing settlement patterns, the
analysis considers whether the observed distribution can be explained by
the broader geography of documented human activity.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis of Archaeological Settlement
Patterns}\label{statistical-analysis-of-archaeological-settlement-patterns}

Similarly, Weber, Stoyan, and Myllymäki apply spatial point-pattern
analysis to investigate relationships between archaeological settlement
distributions across multiple historical periods
(\citeproc{ref-weber2016statistical}{Weber, Stoyan, and Myllymäki
2016}). Their research demonstrates how the spatial association,
continuity, and separation of archaeological site distributions can be
evaluated quantitatively rather than relying exclusively on visual
interpretation. Although their study focuses on archaeological
settlement patterns rather than military engagements, it illustrates the
value of treating archaeological locations as spatial distributions that
can be compared and evaluated statistically. This provides a useful
parallel for examining whether Roman battle locations exhibit patterns
of clustering and geographic association beyond those expected from
alternative spatial distributions.

\section{Data and Study Area}\label{data-and-study-area}

\subsection{Roman Battle Locations}\label{roman-battle-locations}

The primary dataset consists of georeferenced battle records associated
with Roman military history. Battle records were collected using
Wikidata Wikidata Contributors (\citeproc{ref-wikidata}{2026}) and
subsequently reviewed to determine whether Rome was involved in each
engagement. The resulting dataset includes battle identifiers, names,
dates, classifications, and associated geographic locations.

Each battle is assigned to one of two classifications:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{External:} Conflicts involving Rome and external opponents.
\item
  \textbf{Civil Conflict:} Conflicts involving internal Roman political
  or military factions.
\end{itemize}

Battle locations are represented using geographic coordinates and
converted into spatial point geometries for subsequent analysis. Because
individual battles may have multiple associated location records, the
analysis distinguishes between battle-location records and unique
battles. Analyses using multiple location records retain the associated
geographic observations, while the later classification-based nearest
neighbor analysis constructs one representative point per unique battle.

\subsection{Historical Roman Territorial
Boundaries}\label{historical-roman-territorial-boundaries}

Historical Roman territorial boundaries were collected to represent the
changing geographic extent of Roman control across nine historical
periods. Boundary reconstructions were obtained from two online
geographic data sources: the \emph{Roman Empire Cultural Shapefiles}
ArcGIS Feature Service ArcGIS Online
(\citeproc{ref-arcgis_roman_empire}{2026}) and the \emph{Roman Empire
Map} project Barnes (\citeproc{ref-roman_empire_map}{2026}).

The ArcGIS Feature Service provided six historical boundary layers. The
available layers were first inspected through the service's metadata to
identify relevant historical reconstructions and their associated
geographic attributes. The selected layers were downloaded as GeoJSON
files using the ArcGIS REST API, with their output coordinate reference
system specified as WGS 84 (EPSG:4326).

Three additional Republican period boundary reconstructions were
obtained from the Roman Empire Map project's GeoJSON dataset. The source
dataset contained multiple historical boundary features, which were
filtered by date and by the \texttt{part} attribute. Only features
representing the complete territorial extent (\texttt{part\ =\ "whole"})
were retained for the years 264 BC, 133 BC, and 44 BC. These selected
boundaries were then converted to EPSG:4326 and saved as individual
GeoJSON files.

The resulting collection contains nine historical boundary snapshots:

Historical snapshot & Year & Data source \\
\midrule\noalign{}
\endhead
\bottomrule\noalign{}
\endlastfoot

After collection, the boundary files were loaded into GeoPandas for
inspection. This included reviewing their feature counts, coordinate
reference systems, geometry types, geographic extents, and geometry
validity. The individual reconstructions were also mapped separately and
overlaid to examine how the geographic extent of Roman territory changed
across the selected periods.

These boundary datasets provide historical approximations of Roman
territorial extent rather than continuous representations of Roman
expansion and contraction. Because the reconstructions are available
only for selected historical dates, they serve as temporal reference
points for the spatial analysis rather than exact representations of
Roman territorial control at every battle date.

\subsection{Settlement Data}\label{settlement-data}

Settlement information is obtained from the Pleiades Gazetteer Bagnall
et al. (\citeproc{ref-pleiades}{2026}), a historical geographic resource
documenting locations associated with the ancient world. The settlement
dataset includes geographic coordinates, feature classifications, and
temporal attributes. Two temporal attributes are particularly important:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \texttt{MINDATE}: Earliest associated date.
\item
  \texttt{MAXDATE}: Latest associated date.
\end{itemize}

These attributes are used to construct historical settlement datasets
corresponding to the nine Roman territorial snapshots. A settlement is
included in a historical period when its recorded date range contains
the year of that snapshot. The settlement dataset also includes
specialized feature classifications, allowing the analysis to
distinguish between general settlements and particular types of
infrastructure. These include ports, temples, forts, amphitheaters, and
theaters.

\subsection{Geographic Study Area}\label{geographic-study-area}

The geographic study area is defined using the Roman territorial
boundary reconstruction from 314 AD. The boundary geometry is combined
and projected into an coordinate reference system centered approximately
on the Mediterranean region. A 500-kilometer buffer is then constructed
around the territorial boundary. This buffer allows for Roman campaigns
deep into territory that they did not control, such as campaigns fought
agaisnt Parthia or Sassanid Iran in territory that they would never
control, but also for earlier periods allow for battles in Spain and
North Africa during the punic wars that would at later periods be
incorporated into the Roman Empire. Settlement records are restricted to
this study area before the primary spatial analyses.

\section{Methodology}\label{methodology}

The analysis is organized as a sequence of spatial investigations
designed to develop increasingly specific explanations for the
geographic distribution of Roman battles. Rather than treating battle
locations as an isolated spatial phenomenon, the analysis progressively
incorporates historical and geographic context to examine the factors
associated with their distribution.

The first stage establishes the degree of spatial clustering among
recorded battles. The second examines whether the distribution of
settlements provides an explanation for this clustering. The third
introduces Roman territorial boundaries to investigate the relationship
between military activity and the geographic limits of Roman control.
Finally, the analysis considers whether different categories of conflict
exhibit distinct relationships with specialized military and civic
infrastructure.

\subsection{Spatial Data Preparation}\label{spatial-data-preparation}

The battle dataset contains geographic records associated with
individual battles. Records with missing or invalid coordinates are
excluded before spatial analysis, and the remaining coordinates are
converted into point geometries. Settlement locations are similarly
restricted to valid point geometries within the defined study area.

All spatial datasets are transformed into a common projected coordinate
reference system to support distance calculations in meters. The study
area is defined using the historical Roman territorial boundary for 314
AD and a 500 km surrounding buffer.

The unit of analysis varies across the investigations. The initial
spatial randomness, settlement, and territorial-boundary analyses use
battle-location records. The final classification-based infrastructure
analysis instead consolidates records belonging to the same battle into
a single representative location. For battles with multiple location
records, the geometries are combined and their centroid is used as the
representative point. This produces a dataset of unique battles for
comparing the spatial clustering of external and civil conflicts.

\subsection{Monte Carlo Simulation
Framework}\label{monte-carlo-simulation-framework}

The primary statistical approach uses Monte Carlo simulations to compare
observed battle distributions against alternative spatial models.

For each model, the observed spatial statistic is compared with the
empirical distribution generated by its corresponding simulations. The
expected statistic is calculated as the mean of the simulated values,
while the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles provide an empirical interval for
evaluating the observed result. The observed-to-expected ratio is
calculated by dividing the observed statistic by the simulated mean. For
distance-based statistics, ratios below 1 indicate that the observed
distances are shorter than expected, while ratios above 1 indicate that
they are longer.

The direction of the Monte Carlo test depends on the research question.
Lower-tail tests are used when evaluating whether observed distances are
unusually short, such as in the settlement-feature proximity analysis.
Two-sided tests are used for the classification-based infrastructure
models, where unusually short and unusually long nearest-neighbor
distances are both relevant.

\subsection{Spatial Clustering}\label{spatial-clustering}

The initial analysis evaluates whether Roman battle locations exhibit
greater spatial clustering than would be expected under spatial
randomness (CSR). Mean nearest-neighbor distance is used as the measure
of spatial concentration. For each observed battle location, a k-d tree
is used to identify the distance to its nearest neighboring battle
location. The mean of these distances provides the observed clustering
statistic.

The CSR null model generates simulated battle locations by uniformly
sampling points within the study area. Each simulation preserves the
number of observed battle-location records. The mean nearest-neighbor
distance is calculated for each simulated distribution and compared with
the observed statistic. Smaller mean distances indicate greater spatial
clustering.

\subsection{Settlement Geography}\label{settlement-geography}

The settlement-based model evaluates whether the geographic distribution
of documented settlements helps explain the spatial concentration of
Roman battles. Because reliable population-density estimates are
difficult to establish for the ancient world, documented settlement
locations are used as an imperfect proxy for the spatial distribution of
human activity.

A Gaussian kernel density estimate (KDE) is fitted to the filtered
Pleiades settlement locations using a bandwidth of 50 km. The resulting
intensity surface is evaluated across a 20 km sampling grid covering the
study area. Grid-cell intensity estimates are normalized into sampling
probabilities, with locations near concentrations of documented
settlements receiving greater probability of selection.

Monte Carlo simulations generate synthetic battle locations by sampling
grid cells according to these probabilities. Each simulation preserves
the number of observed battle locations and is used to calculate an
expected mean nearest-neighbor distance. This provides a
settlement-conditioned baseline for evaluating whether the observed
clustering of battles can be explained by the distribution of known
settlements.

For the historical boundary analysis, separate settlement intensity
surfaces are also constructed for each of the nine historical boundary
snapshots. Settlement records are assigned to a snapshot using their
Pleiades temporal attributes, retaining locations whose recorded date
ranges include the snapshot year.

The settlement-based model is also applied separately to external
battles and civil conflicts. These classification-specific simulations
preserve the number of observed battle locations in each group and
provide separate expected nearest-neighbor distances for comparison with
the corresponding observed distributions. The combined results provide a
general settlement-based baseline for evaluating the
infrastructure-weighted models.

\subsection{Territorial Boundaries}\label{territorial-boundaries}

The third stage introduces historical territorial boundaries to examine
the relationship between Roman military activity and the geographic
extent of Roman control. Battle locations are compared with the nearest
historical territorial perimeter corresponding to their assigned
chronological period. Their observed distances from these boundaries are
evaluated against distances generated by settlement-based simulations.
This approach examines whether the proximity of battles to territorial
boundaries can be explained by the underlying distribution of
settlements, or whether battles exhibit an additional concentration near
the boundaries of Roman territory.

Territorial boundary association is examined by calculating the shortest
distance between each observed battle location and the corresponding
historical Roman territorial perimeter.

\begin{figure}

\centering{

\includegraphics{visualization/Historicalperiodssnapshot.png}

}

\caption{\label{fig-historicalborderssnapshot}Snapshots of historical
borders used for territorial perimeters.}

\end{figure}%

For each battle, the applicable perimeter is determined using its
assigned historical boundary snapshot. Each battle is assigned to the
closest available historical boundary snapshot based on its date. The
analysis uses nine historical snapshots, ranging from 264 BC to 314 AD.
This approach allows each battle to be evaluated against the territorial
perimeter that most closely represents its chronological context. The
observed mean distance to the historical perimeter is then compared
against simulated distributions. Two alternative approaches are
evaluated.

First, the settlement-based model is used to generate simulated battle
locations, with their distances to the corresponding historical
perimeters establishing the expected boundary distances under a
settlement-conditioned null model. A complementary set of simulations
uses exponential distance-decay functions to generate spatial
distributions increasingly concentrated around territorial boundaries.
These boundary-weighted distributions allow different levels of border
concentration to be compared with the observed geographic pattern.
Specifically, exponential decay is used to construct spatial
distributions at varying levels of concentration around the historical
perimeters:

\[
w(d) = e^{-d/\lambda}
\]

where:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \(d\) is the distance from a grid cell to the corresponding
  territorial perimeter.
\item
  \(\lambda\) is the distance-decay parameter.
\end{itemize}

Four decay distances are evaluated:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  25 kilometers
\item
  50 kilometers
\item
  100 kilometers
\item
  200 kilometers
\end{itemize}

Smaller decay parameters concentrate simulated locations more strongly
near the territorial perimeter.

The observed boundary distances are compared against the resulting
simulated distributions to evaluate whether the observed patterns are
consistent with different levels of territorial boundary association.

For the classification-based comparison, the border-weighted simulations
are also conducted separately for external battles and civil conflicts.
Each simulation preserves the observed number of battle-location records
within each classification and historical period. The period-specific
observed and simulated mean distances are then pooled across the full
historical dataset using the number of battle-location records in each
period as weights. This allows the overall results to account for
differences in the number of recorded battles across historical periods
while maintaining separate estimates for each conflict classification.

\subsection{Military and Civic
Infrastructure}\label{military-and-civic-infrastructure}

The final stage examines whether the geographic distribution of battles
is associated with specialized settlement infrastructure and whether
these relationships differ by conflict classification. The analysis
considers ports, temples, forts, and entertainment facilities, with
amphitheatres and theatres grouped into that single category. Two
separate approaches are used. The first evaluates whether battles are
unusually close to particular infrastructure features relative to the
broader settlement distribution. The second examines whether
infrastructure-weighted spatial distributions can reproduce the observed
clustering of external battles and civil conflicts.

The selection of these infrastructure categories is informed by the
preceding feature-proximity analysis, which identifies differences in
the relationships between conflict classifications and specialized
settlement features. Forts are used to represent military infrastructure
relevant to external battles, while entertainment facilities provide the
corresponding civic infrastructure category for civil conflicts.

\subsubsection{Proximity to Specialized Settlement
Features}\label{proximity-to-specialized-settlement-features}

The first analysis evaluates the proximity of battle locations to four
categories of settlement infrastructure: ports, temples, forts, and
entertainment facilities. For each category, the nearest-feature
distance is calculated for every observed battle location. The observed
mean distance is then compared with a conditional settlement-based Monte
Carlo model.

To prevent the feature being tested from directly influencing its own
background distribution, the corresponding feature category is excluded
from the settlement dataset before fitting the KDE. A settlement
intensity surface is then constructed from the remaining settlement
locations. This surface provides the sampling probabilities used to
generate simulated battle locations, representing the expected
distribution of battles based on the broader settlement geography rather
than the infrastructure category being tested.

The number of simulated locations matches the observed number of battle
locations in each classification. This process is repeated 999 times for
each feature category and classification, producing a simulated
distribution of mean distances to the nearest feature.

The observed mean distance is compared with the simulated distribution
using a lower-tail Monte Carlo test. This evaluates whether battles are
unusually close to a particular infrastructure category relative to what
would be expected from the broader settlement distribution. The
observed-to-expected ratio is calculated by dividing the observed mean
distance by the mean of the simulated distances. Ratios below 1 indicate
closer proximity than expected, while ratios above 1 indicate greater
distances than expected.

\subsubsection{Classification-Based Infrastructure
Models}\label{classification-based-infrastructure-models}

A separate simulation model examines whether the spatial clustering of
external battles and civil conflicts is consistent with distributions
weighted toward different types of infrastructure. External battles are
modeled using proximity to forts, while civil conflicts are modeled
using proximity to entertainment facilities. This approach differs from
the preceding analysis because it evaluates the clustering of battles
relative to one another, rather than their individual distances to
infrastructure features.

The analysis first consolidates multiple location records belonging to
the same battle into a single representative point. For battles with
multiple location records, the geometries are combined and their
centroid is used as the representative location. This produces a dataset
of 332 unique battles, consisting of 252 external battles and 80 civil
conflicts. The observed mean nearest-neighbor distance is then
calculated separately for external battles, civil conflicts, and the
combined dataset.

A shared 20 km sampling grid is constructed across the study area. For
each grid cell, the distance to the nearest relevant infrastructure
location is calculated. These distances are converted into sampling
weights using an exponential distance-decay function:

\[
w(d) = e^{-(d-d_{\min})/\lambda}
\]

where \(d\) represents the distance from a grid cell to the nearest
relevant infrastructure feature, \(d_{\min}\) is the minimum distance
across the sampling grid, and \(\lambda\) is the distance-decay
parameter. Subtracting the minimum distance ensures that the highest
sampling weight is 1. Smaller decay parameters concentrate simulated
locations more strongly around the selected infrastructure, while larger
parameters produce broader spatial distributions.

Four decay parameters are evaluated: 25, 50, 100, and 200 km. For each
parameter, 999 simulations are generated using the corresponding
infrastructure-weighted sampling distribution. Each simulated
classification contains the same number of unique battles as its
observed group: 252 external battles and 80 civil conflicts. Simulated
locations are sampled from the weighted grid, with a small amount of
spatial jitter applied within each grid cell to avoid placing all
simulated points at identical cell locations.

The mean nearest-neighbor distance is calculated separately for each
simulated classification. The simulated external and civil-conflict
locations are also combined to calculate the corresponding statistic for
all battles. This combined result represents the clustering produced by
the two separately simulated classification groups rather than a single
distribution weighted toward one infrastructure category.

Finally, the infrastructure-weighted results are compared with a general
settlement-based baseline. This baseline uses the broader settlement
distribution to generate simulated battle locations without weighting
them toward a specific infrastructure category. Comparing the two models
provides a way to evaluate whether proximity to forts and entertainment
facilities offers a better explanation of the observed spatial
clustering than settlement geography alone.

\subsubsection{Representative Spatial
Simulations}\label{representative-spatial-simulations}

To illustrate the spatial patterns generated by the
infrastructure-weighted models, representative simulations are selected
from the 25 km decay model. For each classification, the simulation
whose mean nearest-neighbor distance is closest to the median of the 999
simulated values is selected. These simulations provide a representative
example of the spatial distributions generated by the model and are
compared with the observed battle locations.

\section{Results}\label{results}

\subsection{Spatial Randomness}\label{spatial-randomness}

The observed distribution of Roman battle locations exhibited
substantially greater spatial clustering than expected under spatial
randomness (CSR). Across 342 battle-location records, the observed mean
nearest-neighbor distance was 41.59 km, compared with an expected mean
distance of 108.70 km under the CSR model. The observed to expected
ratio was 0.383, indicating that the observed mean distance was
approximately 61.7\% lower than expected under spatial randomness.

None of the 999 simulated CSR distributions produced a mean
nearest-neighbor distance as extreme as the observed value. The
resulting Monte Carlo p-value was 0.001, providing strong evidence
against the hypothesis that battle locations were randomly distributed
across the study area.

of simulated mean nearest-neighbor distances under CSR, with the
observed statistic indicated by a vertical reference line. The position
of the observed value relative to the simulated distribution illustrates

\subsection{Border Based Model}\label{border-based-model}

The simulated expected mean distance increased as the distance-decay

model.

distribution most consistent with the observed average distance from
battles to territorial boundaries.

In contrast to the settlement-based model, which found that battle

\subsubsection{Civil versus External
Conflict}\label{civil-versus-external-conflict}

The pooled results show a substantial difference between the two
conflict classifications. Across the 83 civil-conflict records, the
weighted observed mean distance to the nearest territorial boundary was
61.17 km. For the 259 external-conflict records, the corresponding
distance was 164.78 km. Thus, external battles occurred, on average,
approximately 2.7 times farther from territorial boundaries than civil
conflicts.

The observed-to-expected ratios also differed between classifications.
For civil conflicts, the ratio declined from 2.85 under the 25 km decay
model to 0.56 under the 200 km model. Under the 100 km model, the ratio
was 0.95, indicating that the observed mean distance was relatively
close to the simulated expectation. In contrast, external battles had
ratios above 1.0 under all four decay assumptions, ranging from 7.71
under the 25 km model to 1.14 under the 200 km model. This indicates
that external battles were consistently farther from territorial
boundaries than the border-weighted simulations predicted.

For all 342 battle-location records combined, the 200 km decay model
produced the closest correspondence between observed and expected mean
distances, with a ratio of 1.03. However, the classification-specific
results suggest that this overall correspondence masks differences
between external and civil conflicts. The 100 km and 200 km models
produced expected distances relatively close to those observed for civil
conflicts, while external battles remained farther from territorial
boundaries than expected under each model.

These results suggest that the spatial relationship between Roman
territorial boundaries and battle locations differed according to
conflict classification. Civil conflicts were generally located closer
to territorial boundaries, whereas external battles were more distant.
This comparison is descriptive and does not establish that the
differences are statistically significant; the period-level simulation
distributions provide additional context for examining how these
relationships varied over time.

evaluated: ports, temples, forts, and entertainment facilities
(amphitheatres and theatres). The analysis included 99 port locations,
54 temple locations, 50 fort locations, and 89 entertainment locations.
Results were evaluated separately for civil conflicts and external
battles.

\paragraph{Civil Conflicts}\label{civil-conflicts}

Civil conflicts exhibited the strongest proximity to entertainment
facilities. The observed mean distance to the nearest entertainment
location was 95.90 km, compared with a simulated expected distance of
229.26 km. The observed-to-expected ratio was 0.42, with a lower-tail
Monte Carlo p-value of 0.001. The observed distance was substantially
below the simulated distribution, indicating that civil conflicts were
unusually close to entertainment facilities relative to the broader
settlement geography.

Civil conflicts were also closer to ports than expected. The observed
mean distance was 134.03 km, compared with an expected distance of
183.53 km. This produced an observed-to-expected ratio of 0.73 and a
p-value of 0.004, providing evidence of unusually close proximity to
port locations.

The results for temples and forts did not show comparable evidence of
unusually close proximity. The observed mean distance to temples was
204.06 km, compared with an expected distance of 217.65 km (ratio =
0.94, p = 0.229). For forts, the observed mean distance was slightly
greater than expected, at 246.42 km compared with 240.74 km (ratio =
1.02, p = 0.634).

Overall, the civil-conflict results indicate that battles were
particularly close to entertainment facilities and ports, while the
results for temples and forts did not indicate unusually close
proximity.

\paragraph{External Battles}\label{external-battles}

External battles also exhibited unusually close proximity to
entertainment facilities. Their observed mean distance was 182.87 km,
compared with an expected distance of 229.57 km. The
observed-to-expected ratio was 0.80, with a lower-tail p-value of 0.002.

A similar pattern appeared for forts. External battles had an observed
mean distance of 210.15 km, compared with an expected distance of 241.12
km (ratio = 0.87, p = 0.005). This indicates that external battles were
closer to forts than expected under the settlement-based simulation.

The temple results were less conclusive. The observed mean distance was
200.74 km, compared with an expected distance of 217.03 km, producing a
ratio of 0.92 and a p-value of 0.051. Although the observed distance was
shorter than expected, the result was just above the conventional 0.05
threshold.

Ports showed a different pattern. External battles were farther from
ports than expected, with an observed mean distance of 218.88 km
compared with an expected distance of 184.55 km. The
observed-to-expected ratio was 1.19, and the lower-tail p-value was
1.000. Thus, the simulations provide no evidence that external battles
were unusually close to ports.

\paragraph{Comparison Between Conflict
Classifications}\label{comparison-between-conflict-classifications}

The results reveal differences in the spatial relationships between
battle classifications and specialized settlement features.
Entertainment facilities were the only category for which both civil
conflicts and external battles showed evidence of unusually close
proximity relative to the settlement-based simulations. However, the
difference between observed and expected distances was substantially
larger for civil conflicts.

Ports and forts displayed contrasting patterns across the two
classifications. Civil conflicts were significantly closer to ports than
expected, whereas external battles were farther away. Conversely,
external battles were significantly closer to forts than expected, while
civil conflicts showed no evidence of unusually close proximity to them.

Temples produced the least conclusive results. Neither classification
showed evidence of unusually close proximity at the 0.05 significance
threshold, although the external-battle result approached that
threshold.

\subsection{Military and Civic
Infrastructure}\label{military-and-civic-infrastructure-1}

\subsubsection{Unique Battle Locations}\label{unique-battle-locations}

The observed mean nearest-neighbor distances were 50.25 km for external
battles, 89.65 km for civil conflicts, and 43.02 km for all battles
combined. These results indicate that external battles were more
spatially concentrated than civil conflicts, while the combined dataset
exhibited the shortest mean nearest-neighbor distance.

\subsubsection{General Settlement
Baseline}\label{general-settlement-baseline}

As established in the preceding settlement-based analysis, battle
locations remained more spatially clustered than expected under a model
accounting for the geographic distribution of known settlements.

The expected mean nearest-neighbor distances were 82.94 km for external
battles, 153.46 km for civil conflicts, and 71.87 km for all battles
combined. The observed-to-expected ratios were 0.606, 0.584, and 0.599,
respectively, with two-sided Monte Carlo p-values of 0.002 for all three
comparisons. These settlement-based results provide the baseline against
which the infrastructure-weighted simulations are compared below.

\subsubsection{Comparison of Settlement and Infrastructure
Models}\label{comparison-of-settlement-and-infrastructure-models}

The infrastructure-weighted simulations produced different results
depending on the decay distance and battle classification. In general,
increasing the decay distance produced less spatially concentrated
simulated patterns, as reflected by progressively larger expected mean
nearest-neighbor distances.

For external battles, the 25 km fort-based model produced an expected
mean nearest-neighbor distance of 39.14 km, compared with the observed
distance of 50.25 km. The observed-to-expected ratio was 1.284, and the
two-sided Monte Carlo p-value was 0.002. In this case, the observed
battles were less tightly clustered than the simulated locations
concentrated around forts.

As the decay distance increased, the expected nearest-neighbor distance
also increased. Under the 50 km model, the expected distance was 62.06
km (ratio = 0.810, p = 0.002). The 100 km and 200 km models produced
expected distances of 87.97 km and 108.65 km, respectively, with
observed-to-expected ratios of 0.571 and 0.463. Both models produced
p-values of 0.002. Thus, the broader fort-weighted distributions
produced simulated battle patterns that were less tightly clustered than
the observed external battles.

Civil conflicts displayed a different pattern. The 25 km
entertainment-based model produced an expected mean nearest-neighbor
distance of 111.21 km, compared with the observed distance of 89.65 km
(ratio = 0.806, p = 0.020). The 50 km model produced an expected
distance of 129.98 km (ratio = 0.690, p = 0.002). Under the 100 km and
200 km models, expected distances increased to 154.72 km and 186.27 km,
respectively. Both models produced observed-to-expected ratios below
0.60 and p-values of 0.002. Across all four decay parameters, the
simulated civil-conflict patterns were less tightly clustered than the
observed distribution.

For all battles combined, the 25 km infrastructure model produced an
expected mean nearest-neighbor distance of 45.23 km, relatively close to
the observed distance of 43.02 km. Its observed-to-expected ratio was
0.951, and the two-sided p-value was 0.304. This was the only model
comparison that did not indicate a statistically unusual difference
between the observed and simulated clustering statistics at the
conventional 0.05 threshold.

The broader infrastructure models produced progressively larger expected
distances. At 50 km, the expected distance was 62.39 km (ratio = 0.690,
p = 0.002). At 100 km and 200 km, expected distances increased to 80.89
km and 96.54 km, with ratios of 0.532 and 0.446, respectively. Both
comparisons produced p-values of 0.002. For comparison, the general
settlement model produced an expected distance of 71.87 km (ratio =
0.599, p = 0.002).

Overall, the results indicate that the relationship between
infrastructure-weighted spatial distributions and observed battle
clustering varied by classification. The 25 km fort-based model
generated a more tightly clustered external-battle pattern than the
observed distribution, while the broader fort-based models generated
less tightly clustered patterns. Civil-conflict simulations consistently
produced less clustered patterns than those observed, although the
difference was smallest under the 25 km entertainment-based model.

The 25 km infrastructure model also produced the closest correspondence
with the observed clustering of all battles combined. This result can be
viewed through the lense of the classification-specific simulations: the
combined model joins separately simulated external battles weighted
toward forts and civil conflicts weighted toward entertainment
facilities. It therefore represents a combination of two
infrastructure-weighted distributions rather than a single probability
surface.

\section{Conclusion}\label{conclusion}

This study examined the spatial geography of Roman warfare by evaluating
the relationship between battle locations, settlement patterns,
territorial boundaries, and specialized infrastructure. Using
battle-location records across multiple historical periods, the analysis
applied spatial statistical methods and Monte Carlo simulations to
investigate whether the distribution of Roman battles could be explained
by broader geographic patterns. The results indicate that Roman battles
were not distributed randomly across the study area. Instead, their
locations exhibited substantial spatial clustering, and this clustering
persisted when the distribution of documented settlements was
incorporated into the statistical models.

The settlement-based analysis demonstrated that the geography of known
settlements provided a better explanation for the distribution of
battles than spatial randomness alone. However, the model still fell
short of reproducing the observed clustering. The expected mean
nearest-neighbor distance decreased substantially when settlement
geography was incorporated, but remained considerably higher than the
observed distance. This suggests that settlement geography provides an
important baseline for understanding the distribution of Roman battles,
but does not fully account for the factors associated with their spatial
concentration.

The territorial-boundary analysis identified another important spatial
relationship. Battle locations were, on average, substantially closer to
historical Roman territorial boundaries than expected under the
settlement-conditioned model. However, the border-weighted simulations
revealed that the strength of this relationship depended on the assumed
distance-decay parameter. The broadest model, using a 200 km decay
distance, produced the closest correspondence with the observed overall
mean distance. This suggests that the geographic relationship between
warfare and Roman territorial boundaries was not necessarily limited to
engagements immediately adjacent to the frontier. Rather, the results
are consistent with a broader zone of military activity surrounding the
territorial limits of Roman control.

The classification-based results further demonstrate that external and
civil conflicts exhibited different spatial relationships with Roman
territorial boundaries. External battles remained farther from the
boundaries than expected under each of the four border-weighted models,
while the civil-conflict results were more closely reproduced by the
broader decay assumptions. This finding differed from the initial
expectation that external conflicts would exhibit the strongest
relationship with territorial boundaries. Instead, the results suggest
that combining all Roman battles into a single spatial distribution can
obscure meaningful variation in the geography of different types of
conflict.

The infrastructure analysis provides additional evidence of these
differences. Civil conflicts exhibited particularly strong proximity to
entertainment facilities and ports relative to the broader settlement
geography. External battles, in contrast, were unusually close to forts
and entertainment facilities, while showing no evidence of unusually
close proximity to ports. The classification-based nearest-neighbor
simulations also demonstrated that the relationship between
infrastructure and battle clustering varied according to the assumed
distance-decay parameter. The 25 km infrastructure model produced the
closest correspondence with the observed clustering of all battles
combined, although this aggregate result concealed differences between
the two classifications. External battles were less tightly clustered
than the fort-weighted simulations under the narrowest decay assumption,
while civil conflicts remained more tightly clustered than their
entertainment-weighted simulations.

Taken together, the findings suggest that the spatial geography of Roman
warfare is more complicated than any single model in this study can
explain. The analyses provide evidence against spatial randomness and
demonstrate that settlement geography improves upon a uniform random
baseline. However, settlement geography alone does not reproduce the
observed concentration of battles. Territorial boundaries and
specialized infrastructure offer additional ways of characterizing this
distribution, but their explanatory value varies across historical
periods, conflict classifications, and model assumptions.

Rather than identifying one definitive geographic explanation for where
Roman battles occurred, this study highlights the limitations of
treating military activity as the product of a single spatial
relationship. The results suggest that Roman warfare was associated with
several overlapping geographic structures, but that the importance of
these structures differed depending on the type and historical context
of the conflict. This leaves open considerable opportunities for further
research into the geographic, political, and strategic factors
underlying the distribution of Roman military engagements.

\subsection{Limitations and Future
Research}\label{limitations-and-future-research}

\subsubsection{Limitations}\label{limitations}

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these
findings. First, the battle dataset is dependent on the availability and
geographic precision of historical records and their representation in
Wikidata. The resulting sample is therefore not a complete inventory of
all Roman military engagements. A cursory look at the battle mappings of
the different historical time period shows centuries where there appear
to be only a few battles recorded most likely due to limited sources as
opposed to those being true times of peace.

This leads to next limitation, the historical boundary reconstructions
provide snapshots of Roman territorial extent rather than continuous
representations of political control. This is particularly important for
the time period of the Roman republic when the borders of the empire
rapidly changed. This may have meaningfully impacted the border analysis
done in this research.

\subsubsection{Future Research}\label{future-research}

The amount of future research opportunities are as exciting as they are
plenty. The most obvious continuation would be to incorporate
geographical features such as rivers, mountains, and more into this
research. Any traditional study of military history will include a lot
many mentions of river crossings or mountain passes where decisive
battles take place, so pulling in that information would bring this
quantitative research in closer proximity to the shoulders of giants
studying Roman history.

Expanding the temporal range of the study would also bring in existing
new possibilities. This study of battles ends slightly after the fall of
the Western Roman Empire, but futher study could be done to include the
campaigns of Belisarius where the Eastern Roman Empire retook North
Africa and Italy all the way until the fall of Constantinople to Mehmed
II.

The most exciting potential area of expansion, would be to pull in
details of the battles or wars themselves through additional data on
WikiData and Wikipedia. Using linked articles of the battles to pull in
additional context either through network analysis or NLP on the
articles itself. This would allow for a finer grain distinction than the
civil versus external conflict in this study and would allow for
groupings of conflicts based on numerous factors.
